StitchCraft

Automatic digitizing

Automatic digitizing makes a machine file from a picture without a setting on any of its shapes. StitchCraft chooses the stitches of each shape from its shape and mends the places where shapes meet. It also orders the sewing for few colour changes. It is made for 2 kinds of picture:

  • Flat-colour art, such as logos, badges, clip art and drawings. Its colours are solid shapes, stacked in layers or abutting.
  • A photo reduced to a few flat colours, such as 4. It sews in those flat colours, as flat-colour art does.

Flat-colour art

Logos, badges, clip art and drawings have a few flat colours, each of them one solid shape or more. The emblem below, docs/fixtures/emblem.svg, is drawn in layers in 4 of the machine’s threads, each layer whole under the layers drawn over it.

Digitized

The navy square and the white disc are broader than a satin column, and each is filled whole under the shapes on it. The bars are 3 mm tall and sew as satin columns. The white round the square is the picture’s background, the fabric’s own colour, and is left out.

To digitize such art:

  • Art drawn in VectorCraft, or opened there from an SVG file, is made of shapes already. Select them, choose Effect › Plug-ins › StitchCraft Auto, and write the machine file (how). On the command line, add --auto to stitch plan or stitch export, as in stitch plan emblem.svg --auto -o emblem.pes.
  • A picture of the art, a PNG or JPEG file, is traced into shapes first, in the machine’s threads. stitch plan emblem.png -o emblem.pes traces it with VectorCraft’s command line, then digitizes the shapes. In VectorCraft, Image Trace traces it (from a picture to a machine file).

A photo in a few colours

A photo’s colours blend into each other. VectorCraft’s Image Trace reduces them to a few flat colours first, such as 4 of the machine’s threads, the backdrop’s colour among them. The traced shapes are then digitized as flat-colour art is. The photo sews in those few flat colours, and its blended shading isn’t reproduced. Image to embroidery leaves photos sewn in blended colours outside the roadmap.

A photo’s background, such as the turf a dog stands on, traces into shapes of its own, which sew too. To sew the subject alone, remove the background first, in any program that makes it transparent, and save the photo as a PNG file. Image Trace leaves transparent pixels out. The photo then traces into its subject’s shapes alone (subject cut-out). Then stitch plan dog.png --colours 9 --size 100 -o dog.pes, or Image Trace in VectorCraft, traces and digitizes it as any photo. The owner’s photos of her dog were made that way. How it chooses the stitches

Automatic digitizing chooses the stitches of each shape without settings. A shape with settings sews as they say. The pictures below show shapes as they are drawn, and then digitized automatically. Without --auto, each of those shapes sews as one tatami fill, whatever its width. Image to embroidery gives every rule in full.

Stitches by shape

Each part of a shape sews by the first rule that it matches. The rules go by the part’s shape alone, never by what the picture shows: a flower and a dog sew by the same rules. The rules’ reference has the table with each bound’s value, and image to embroidery says why each rule is there.

  • A line. A part nowhere wider than the design’s narrowest satin stroke runs along its middle, as a triple run when it is wide enough to show.
  • An area. A part wider than the widest satin column somewhere is a tatami fill.
  • A blob. A part no wider than a satin column, and short for its width, is a small tatami fill. A column along its middle would sew its stitches into a fan round a point. The smallest blob is a dot.
  • A ragged patch. A part that branches, and whose width swells and shrinks along its branches, is a tatami fill too. Sewn as a column along each branch, its edge would be scalloped. Its thin tails leave the fill and run.
  • A column. Any other part sews as satin columns between its own edges, as a letter, a leaf, a ring and a tapering stroke do.

A satin stitch longer than 7 mm lies loose, so a part whose column would sew one is filled instead. A part that is filled is filled whole under the shapes that fill its holes, and they sew on it.

A letter’s counter, such as the hole in an A or an O, shows the fabric or the field under it where a disc 0.8 mm across fits in it and nothing drawn later lies over it: the columns round it end their stitches on its edge, without the pull compensation that widens them elsewhere. A smaller counter closes under its own threads. Straight columns keep their edges straight, and where a column bends or fans, the stitches that crowd on the inside of the bend are shortened.

The pictures are drawn from docs/fixtures/rules.svg. Its 5 shapes are a blob, a letter T of a thick and a thin stroke, a blob with 2 tails, a nose with 2 nostrils and a leaf.

As drawn

Digitized

The T shows the rule’s limit. Each of its strokes is even, but they are unlike each other, so the letter as a whole counts as ragged and is filled. For art drawn in strokes of changing thickness, turn Fill uneven parts off, or use the logo style (changing the choice), and the T sews as 2 satin columns.

A small star is filled too. Its centre line runs from one point to the point opposite, and one satin column along that line would sew the other points as bumps on its sides. StitchCraft measures how far a shape lies from its centre line on both sides, and fills one that lies much farther out than a column does. A cross with square arms sews a column along each arm, and a letter V keeps its columns: its corner lies on one side only.

Each fill sews an outline: a running stitch round its edge in its own thread, sewn after its rows. The outline draws the points of a small shape, which the ends of a fill’s rows leave blunt, and its stitches are shorter where the edge bends, so that the outline of a round shape is round. It sews where the edge shows, and not along an edge that a shape sewn later covers. To sew without outlines, turn Outline round fills off in the Auto effect’s dialog, or on the command line: stitch plan badge.svg --auto -o badge.pes --set auto_fill_outline=false. A traced photo then sews about a tenth fewer stitches, and leaves a few jump threads fewer to cut.

Stitches by width

A line, a column and an area differ by their width. A shape’s width at a place is the diameter of the largest circle that fits inside it there. A stroke is a line as wide as it is drawn, and its width chooses its stitches: one as wide as a satin column sews as a satin column drawn as one path. The pictures are drawn from docs/fixtures/widths.svg, a line 0.6 mm wide, a letter C 3 mm wide and a disc 16 mm across, each traced as an area.

Neighbours

The shapes of a traced picture meet, and sewn without --auto they sew badly there. Digitizing mends that in 6 steps:

  1. The background. The lowest shape, when it covers the whole picture with the other shapes drawn on it, is the background, the fabric’s own colour, and is left out (SC-W0906, and auto_ignore_background to keep it). What shows of it inside the picture still sews, such as a white disc on a badge whose colours are stacked.
  2. Layers. VectorCraft’s Image Trace draws a picture’s colours abutting, each area with a hole where the next one lies, or stacked, each colour whole under the colours drawn over it. Each shape sews only what shows of it, and a picture sews alike whichever way it was traced. A broad area with details on it, such as dots on a flower’s middle, is filled whole. It sews first, and its details sew on top. Outlines and letters, no wider than a satin column (auto_max_satin_width_mm), keep their holes, and the shapes inside them sew beside them (SC-I0908).
  3. Thin lines. A photo traces soft shading and the strands of fur as thin, ragged lines between patches. Each would sew as a running stitch that the fills beside it end their rows on. So a part narrower than a satin column can be, whose width varies along it, sews as part of the fill of the shape it shares the longest edge with (SC-I0915). A line of even width, as a pen draws it, sews as a line. Turn Fill uneven parts off (auto_fill_uneven) to keep every line, as the logo style does. A line that doesn’t touch another piece of its colour, such as a twig or the pale rim of a petal, is a detail of its own, and merged it is lost. So lines merge only while the picture keeps a share of its detail, Least likeness kept when lines merge (auto_min_likeness), those of the colours with the most pieces first, and the rest sew as lines. The default, 0.85, keeps 0.75 or more of every picture measured. Each line kept sews as a running stitch and can add a jump thread: the photos measured sewed 61 to 274 more shapes as running stitches and 50 to 236 more jump threads. Lower the setting for fewer jump threads where the fine lines matter less, such as a photo sewn small, or set it to 0 to merge every line. Keeping lines changes the order the shapes sew in. The cap on thread (auto_thick_cap_fills) holds every spot to 4 fills in any order.
  4. Specks. A shape or a part of one smaller than 1 mm² would sew a knot. It is merged into the shape it shares the longest edge with (SC-I0905, auto_min_area_mm2).
  5. Overlaps. The thread pulls stitches in from the edges of their shape, so shapes that only meet show fabric between them once sewn. Each area reaches 1 mm under the shapes sewn after it that it touches (SC-I0904, auto_overlap_mm). An area filled whole under its details is cut away under them, 1 mm inside their edges, so that each place sews once. A satin column sews 1 mm past a side that a shape sewn after it runs along, and 0.4 mm past any other side (auto_pull_compensation_mm).
  6. The base layer. One open underlay sews first under the whole design (covering the fabric).

The pictures are drawn from docs/fixtures/traced-badge.svg, a badge traced on white with its colours abutting. Without --auto, the white round the badge sews too, and each colour sews up to the next with no overlap.

Covering the fabric

You can’t choose the colour of the fabric a design is sewn on, and on black fabric every place the thread leaves bare is a dark spot. So a design digitized automatically sews one base layer first, under all of it: an open fill of stitches 3 mm long, in the thread that the picture has the most of. Its rows run across the rows of the fills on it. For a towel or a fleece, auto_base_rows set to lattice sews it in 3 directions, which holds the pile down and lays 3 times the thread. It lies 0.4 mm inside the design’s outline, so that it doesn’t show beside the edge. The fills sewn on it sew no underlay of their own (SC-I0909).

With the base layer and the overlap of 1 mm, the pear and the traced dogs cover the fabric inside their outlines. Where thin lines of fur meet, the top thread still leaves a sliver here and there, and the base layer’s thread shows in it in place of the fabric. The running dog above has none in 1,944 mm² of design, and the same dog in 6 threads has 1, of 0.26 mm². With the base layer that dog sews 2,112 stitches fewer than with an underlay under each fill, and the pear 199 fewer.

To sew without the base layer, turn auto_base_layer off. Each fill then sews its own underlay, and StitchCraft says that fabric may show between shapes (SC-I0911). The settings also set the base layer’s stitch length, whether it keeps the design’s holes, and, among the advanced ones, its offset. 2 more are limits that StitchCraft’s own checks hold a design to, and nothing sews differently by them: the largest sliver, 0.4 mm², and the most the top thread may leave to the base layer, 0.5 % of the design. The reference machine hasn’t sewn a base layer yet, and MC-8 sews the dog on black fabric with it and without.

How thick it sews

Shapes that overlap put thread on thread, and a needle that has to go through too much of it deflects and shreds or breaks the thread. StitchCraft measures every design it digitizes: how many fills of thread lie over each spot, and how many times the needle goes down in each square millimetre. One fill is what a tatami fill lays by default. When a place of 1 mm² or more has 4 fills or more, or 15 needle points or more, stitch plan warns (SC-W0913) and still writes the file. The warning names the thickest spot and the shapes stacked there.

StitchCraft aims to keep every spot at 3.25 fills or less, a limit called the cap. Once the design is planned, a last pass and then the cap take thread away at each spot above it. They take thread that other stitches cover, first under the shapes sewn later and then where a shape lays its stitches twice over the same place. They take only the stitches of the shapes they digitized. Below 4 fills they leave a satin column’s top stitches and the rows under its edge. A column keeps its edge and its angle. What shows keeps its colour, and no jump thread or thread change is added. SC-I1119 says what they took and what is left above the cap, which is mostly where 2 satin columns meet over a fill.

Above 4 fills, the warning’s limit (auto_thick_warn_fills), a spot is too thick to sew well. There the last pass and the cap take the rows under a column’s edge too, and what is still above 4 fills then loses a column’s top stitches, one at a time, those that change the column least, until the spot is at 4 fills. The warning comes only where a place of 1 mm² has 4 fills or more. The advanced setting auto_thick_cap_fills moves the cap.

stitch preview dog.pes --style thickness -o thick.png draws this picture of any machine file. Grey is thread under 2 fills, yellow is 2 or more, orange 4 or more and red 6 or more. On the dog above, a few specks are orange: its thickest spot has 4.0 fills, and 94 mm² of 1,944 mm² has 3 fills or more, where it had 177 mm² with the cap at 4. Before StitchCraft sewed an area’s overlap by the way its rows run, and one overlap alone under a shape, 629 mm² was orange or red, and M13.30 left 280 mm² and a thickest spot of 7.0 fills.

The limits are starting values for a 75/11 needle and 40-weight polyester thread. They come from the public advice of machine and software makers, who disagree. The reference machine hasn’t sewn a design to confirm them yet. Until MC-8 has, sew a test piece of the thickest part before a whole design, slowly, and listen for the needle thumping. The settings auto_thick_warn_fills and the 3 beside it move the limits.

Sewing order

The shapes sew in an order StitchCraft chooses. 6 rules decide it, each where the rules before it leave a choice:

  1. Of 2 shapes that overlap, the lower sews first, as drawn.
  2. A running line sews after a fill it borders, and covers its edge.
  3. The thread changes as seldom as rules 1 and 2 allow. On a machine with one needle, each change is a rethread by hand. Each thread sews every shape it can before the next thread starts, and StitchCraft searches for the row of threads with the fewest changes.
  4. A satin column sews after a fill it borders where that costs no thread change. Where it sews first, it reaches 1 mm under the fill.
  5. A detail, such as an eye or a mark on a patch, sews after the larger shapes round it where that costs no thread change, so that no fill’s rows lie over it. Any shape whose neighbours close in on it and are larger counts as one. StitchCraft finds it by its outline and its neighbours alone, not by what the picture shows. The rule is off by default. It costs no thread change, but on traced photos it leaves 1 to 7 more jump threads to cut by hand and shows no more of the picture. Turn it on with --set auto_details_last=true when no fill’s rows may lie over the edge of an eye or a mark, as on a logo or a drawing with small marks on large patches. The cap on thread still limits every spot to 4 fills.
  6. Of the shapes a thread may sew, the nearest sews next.

One thread or another comes back where the rules need it. Dark outlines sew after the light fills they border, and a dark fill under those sews before them. A picture in 6 threads changes thread 5 times at least. Traced from a photo in 6 threads, a dog changes thread 8 times, and in 9 threads 12 times. Fewer thread changes leave more jump threads to cut, because the machine cuts the thread at a change and not at a jump. stitch plan says how often the thread changes, and SC-I0907 compares the order with paint order.

Jump threads

Between 2 shapes of one thread that don’t touch, the needle jumps. A machine with one needle, such as the reference machine, leaves the thread of a jump for you to cut. Where shapes sewn later cover a way from one shape to the next, StitchCraft sews a run along it instead: stitches 2.5 mm long at most, 0.4 mm or more inside what covers them, which you won’t see once those shapes are sewn. The run goes round a place where it would show, as long as that is no farther than twice the jump and 10 mm. A run under the outline that follows a fill, in the fill’s own thread, lies where the outline will.

What is left are the jumps that nothing sewn later covers:

  • the jumps of the thread that sews last, and of the lines that sew after the fills they border
  • a jump across a shape sewn earlier, or across bare fabric
  • a jump whose end lies more than 0.8 mm from the nearest place that a later shape covers
  • the jumps between shapes with settings of their own, which sew as you set them

A traced dog leaves 60 to 83 jump threads where it left 73 to 96. The traced pear keeps its 2, to the stem and back over the background, which sews nothing. A stitch shorter than 3 mm from one shape to the next stays a stitch, as before. Where it would show on another colour for 1 mm or more, it becomes a run if a way is covered. The runs are always on. SC-I0914 says when a design needs more work than the budget allows to look for them, and then sews with its jumps.

Settings by width

Each satin column digitizing makes has the underlay of its width, and 0.4 mm of pull compensation:

Below 2 mmCentre walk
2 to 4 mmContour
Above 4 mmContour and zigzag

Fills keep a tatami fill’s defaults, but sew no underlay of their own on the base layer, and turn from row to row in a stitch at the outline. These are starting values, which the next sew-out at the machine checkpoint confirms or changes.

Text

A text that is still text in the drawing sews as lettering in an embroidery font, whose letters were digitized one by one for the needle. An SVG file’s text is such a text, and so is point type in a VectorCraft document. The word stands where the drawing has it, on its baseline and about as tall, in the text’s colour.

Its letters are not those of the drawing’s typeface. StitchCraft can’t read a typeface’s letter shapes yet, so every text comes out in the embroidery font’s letters, which are light and upright in Ink/Stitch Small Font. A bold text sews in Barstitch Bold, whose strokes are half again as wide, when its font size is 51 points (17.9 mm) or more. A smaller bold text sews in Small Font, and the report says from what size it would sew bold. Small Font’s letters are wider than most typefaces’, so a line at the text’s height would often run past the shape it is drawn on. StitchCraft keeps each line to the length the text most likely had in the drawing: half the font size for each character, or the length an SVG file states with textLength. It closes the letters up a little, then sews the line smaller. The report says for each text which font was used, how tall it sewed and why, and how long its longest line is (SC-I1116):

STITCHCRAFT_FONTS=~/embroidery-fonts stitch plan badge.svg --auto -o badge.pes

stitch finds the font in the folder STITCHCRAFT_FONTS names, and VectorCraft’s exporters have Small Font built in. Without a font the text is left out, and SC-W1111 says so.

  • To keep the typeface’s look, make paths of the text in the drawing program, with Path › Object to Path in Inkscape or Type › Create Outlines in VectorCraft. The letters then sew as shapes.
  • To choose the embroidery font and its size in VectorCraft, give the text StitchCraft’s Lettering effect (letter in VectorCraft).
  • To sew the design without the text, hide or delete the text in the drawing.

Small Font sews letters from 3.6 to 15.2 mm tall, which is text from about 15 to 61 points. Smaller and larger text sews at the nearest of those heights, and SC-W1106 says so. A line that is still too long at 3.6 mm sews at 3.6 mm, and SC-W1117 says by how much it is longer than the text’s place. Text on a path, vertical text and VectorCraft’s area type are left out with a warning.

Changing the choice

Each shape says which rule chose its stitches, with the numbers that decided it (SC-I0912, in the report of stitch plan --report). The settings that change the choice come in 3 tiers:

  • For a shape, Stitch (auto_stitch) names its stitch: a running stitch, satin columns or a tatami fill. A part that can’t sew so goes by the rules.
  • For a design, Satin or fill (auto_prefer) leans the rules towards satin columns or towards fills, Fill uneven parts (auto_fill_uneven) turns the filling of ragged patches off, Fill angle (auto_fill_angle) turns every fill’s rows, and Outline round fills (auto_fill_outline) turns the outlines off. In VectorCraft, apply StitchCraft’s Auto effect to the layer or the group, and set them in its dialog.
  • Advanced, each rule’s bound is a setting too, such as the least slenderness of a column, and so is the base layer’s offset (auto_base_offset_mm). No dialog shows these. On the command line, --set sets one on every shape digitized automatically: stitch plan photo.jpg -o photo.pes --set auto_min_slenderness=4.

A style is a named set of these settings. stitch plan --style logo suits flat art drawn in strokes: it prefers satin columns, and fills no uneven part and merges no thin line. --style photo is the defaults. The rules’ reference lists what each style sets, and every setting with its tier.

Settings

Each rule goes by the settings of the shape it digitizes, and a shape that sets none takes the defaults, as every shape does with --auto. VectorCraft’s Auto effect sets them, and the reference gives each one’s range and default. The narrowest satin column is the design’s min_satin_stroke_width_mm, and the underlay of a column’s width is no setting.
